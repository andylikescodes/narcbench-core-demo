\large A behavioural study of stake, wish and intention in Gemma-2-9B-it}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent We put four copies of Gemma-2-9B-it on a review committee, give all four the same system prompt, and do not
tell them to collude. Each pair talks in private, the committee discusses in the open, and each member records a vote.
One of the two options is the one the members privately gain from. In five rounds (\SN{episodes} episodes) we vary what
the prompt says about that gain. \textbf{A stake stated as a fact never moves a vote} (\V{FACT}{tv} of \V{FACT}{n}).
A wish or a heavier loss moves some votes, mostly of members who act alone and argue for the other option in public.
\textbf{One sentence that states an intention is the step}: \V{T7}{pct}\% of votes and collusion in
\V{T7}{colpct}\% of rooms, repeated on all 50 scenarios (\V{T7b}{pct}\% and \V{T7b}{colpct}\%).
\textbf{The scenario matters as much as the prompt}: under the intention every vote goes to the stake option in
\N{scenAll} scenarios and none in \N{scenZero}. The idea of backing the stake option comes up in private almost
everywhere; what differs is whether the partner goes along. \textbf{Much of that difference is the kind of stake}: given
to the same twelve cases, a stake that an office above the member controls draws \SN{kOnePct}\% of votes to the stake
option, and a plain payout \SN{kTwoPct}\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{The episode.} A committee of four must choose between two options of a case from the NARCBench Core
bank~\cite{rose2026narcbench}: 50 cases from 17 domains, such as a hospital ethics board, a zoning board or an audit
committee. All four members are Gemma-2-9B-it~\cite{gemma2} and get the same system prompt; only the name differs. An
episode is 24 calls: two private rooms of two members with four alternating messages each, three open rounds in which
every member speaks, and a recorded vote. Replies are sampled at temperature 0.7. One option is the \emph{stake option}:
the one tied to an outside interest from which the members can gain. The bank marks the other option as the fair choice.

\paragraph{What varies.} The system prompt is a role line and, depending on the type, a short paragraph about the stake
(Table~\ref{tab:types}). The paragraph starts from two facts, what an outside party wants and what the member gains or
loses, and adds one sentence at a time: a heavier loss, a wish (``You would like the decision to go in favour of that
interest. You have not decided what to do about it.''), an intention (``You intend to see the decision go in favour of
that interest.''). No type except the control says what to do, argue or hide, and none mentions the room partner.

\paragraph{What is measured.} The \emph{vote} is read from the recommendation line. Each \emph{private room} is coded
by two coders, Gemma-2-9B-it and Claude, who see neither the type nor the votes: a pact for the stake option, a pact
on the merits, a split, or no pact (kappa \N{kappaTwo}, \N{kappaThree}, \N{kappaFour} and \SN{kappaFive} in rounds 2
to 5). A room is \emph{collusion} when both coders read a pact for the stake option, both members vote for it and neither
tells the committee; it is \emph{clean} when both read a pact on the merits or no pact and both vote for the other
option. The \emph{public position}, the option a member argues for in the open rounds, is coded the same way and set
against the vote.

\paragraph{Rounds.} Rounds 1 to 3 try 16 prompt types on 12 scenarios with two runs each. Round 4 runs the intention
on all 50 scenarios, with a no-stake baseline. Round 5 gives 12 scenarios a stake of each of two kinds, four runs each.
In all: \SN{episodes} episodes, \SN{calls} calls, \SN{votes} votes, all of them readable, \$\SN{costAll} on one A40; a
first pod of round 5 cost another \$0.90, and its results were lost in the download. The prompts, the readings and the
decision rules of each round were written down before it ran.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{1. A stake stated as a fact moves no vote.} Five types state the stake as a fact: with and without a
paragraph that tells the member to judge on the merits, with a rule that makes coordination pay, and with option texts
stripped of any judgement. Not one of \V{FACT}{n} votes goes to the stake option. In private the members tell each other
what they have heard and agree to decide on the merits (``\Q{transparent}''). Without any stake, \V{T0}{tv} of \V{T0}{n} votes go to the stake
option, so the bank's cases do not lean towards it by themselves.

\paragraph{2. A stated intention is the step.} A heavier loss or a wish moves \V{T2}{pct}\% to \V{T6}{pct}\% of votes
and produces almost no pacts: at most one room in 48 is collusion (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}). One sentence that states an
intention gives
\V{T7}{pct}\% of votes and collusion in \V{T7}{col} of \V{T7}{rooms} rooms, while \V{T7}{clean} rooms under the same
prompt stay clean. The benchmark's own instruction to collude gives \V{I1}{pct}\%, so being told is not far above
intending. The intention sentence gives the member an aim. The pact with the partner and the silence towards the
committee are not in the prompt; the model adds them: ``\Q{discreet}''

\paragraph{3. Below the intention, members act alone and hide the vote.} The last column of Table~\ref{tab:types}
counts the votes for the stake option that come from members who argued for the \emph{other} option in the open
discussion. Under the loss and the wish that is most of them (\PB{T2}{other} of \PB{T2}{sv}, \PB{T3}{other} of
\PB{T3}{sv}, \PB{T6}{other} of \PB{T6}{sv}); under the intention about half (\PB{T7b}{other} of \PB{T7b}{sv}); under the
instruction none. The weaker the nudge, the more the vote is hidden. A member that is told to collude argues for the
stake option openly. In one episode under the intention (\sid{food\_01}, a restaurant's failed health inspection) all
four members argue in the open rounds for upholding the failing score, by both coders' reading, and all four then vote
to reduce it.

\paragraph{4. It repeats, and the scenario matters as much as the prompt.} On all 50 scenarios the intention gives
\V{T7b}{pct}\% of votes and collusion in \V{T7b}{col} of \V{T7b}{rooms} rooms, and the 12 scenarios of round 3 come
out in the same order again (rank correlation \SN{rankRepeat}). But the scenarios split (Figure~\ref{fig:scen}): every
vote goes to the stake option in \N{scenAll} of them and none in \N{scenZero}. Table~\ref{tab:funnel} shows where they part. The idea comes up
almost everywhere: even where nobody votes for the stake option, a member proposes it in private in most rooms. What
differs is whether the other member goes along. Scenarios that give both a collusion room and a clean room
(\N{scenBoth} of 50) are simply those in which that answer can go either way.

\paragraph{5. What separates the scenarios.} Two blind coders rated properties of the 50 scenario texts. We chose the
properties after seeing the results, so this part describes and does not test. Votes for the stake option are
\SN{pPublic}\% where the committee is a public body (\SN{pPublicN} scenarios), \SN{pProf}\% in professional bodies and
\SN{pCompany}\% inside companies. They are \SN{pAbove}\% where an office above the member controls what the member has
at stake, and \SN{pAuto}\% where the stake pays out automatically; \SN{pLoss}\% where the member would lose something
it holds, and \SN{pGain}\% where it would gain something new. A law or rule cited in the case makes no clear difference
(\SN{pRuleDiff} points, interval \SN{pRuleLo} to \SN{pRuleHi}), and neither does the gravity of the stake option:
``Deny and deport (political pressure)'' gets 8 of 8 votes.

In words: the model goes along where the committee is a public body and an office that controls the member's post
wants the outcome, and it declines where the member would simply be paid. But these properties come together, and the
stake sentences are ours, written case by case, so part of what looks like the scenario may be our wording.

\paragraph{6. On the same cases, the kind of stake moves a third of the votes.} Round 5 tests this. Twelve cases, six
from each end of round~4, are each run four times with the stake written in each of two kinds, on one sentence pattern
per kind. \emph{From above}: ``\dots that [an office] wants [the outcome]. [The office] decides whether your post is
renewed, and the renewal comes up this year.'' \emph{Payout}: ``\dots that [the value of a party] depends on [the
outcome]. You hold [a holding] that pays you about \$100,000 if [the outcome].'' Everything else is the intention prompt.
The cases, the 24 texts and two decision rules were fixed before the run.

\qa{The kind of stake matters.} Under a stake from above \SN{kOnePct}\% of votes go to the stake option, under a payout
\SN{kTwoPct}\%: \SN{kDiff} points on the same cases (95\% interval \SN{kLo} to \SN{kHi}), higher in \SN{kHigher} of the
12 cases and lower in \SN{kLower} (Table~\ref{tab:kinds}). The six cases in which nobody voted for the stake option in
round 4 give \SN{kEndZeroOne}\% under a stake from above. The six in which everybody did give \SN{kEndAllTwo}\% under a
payout. Rooms labelled collusion are \SN{kOneCol} of \SN{kOneRooms} against \SN{kTwoCol} of \SN{kTwoRooms}. As in
Result~4, the difference is made where the other member answers a proposal: it goes along in \SN{kOneAcc} rooms under
a stake from above and in \SN{kTwoAcc} under a payout. Under a payout the vote is also hidden throughout:
\SN{kTwoHidden} of the \SN{kTwoStakeVotes} votes for the stake option come from members who argued for the other
option in public.

\qa{The case seems to matter too, and the rule for it is not met.} Under the same kind of stake the two groups of
cases still differ: \SN{kCaseOneAll}\% against \SN{kCaseOneZero}\% from above, \SN{kCaseTwoAll}\% against
\SN{kCaseTwoZero}\% for a payout. The rule fixed in advance asked for an interval that excludes zero under both kinds,
and with six cases per group both intervals reach down to zero (\SN{kCaseOneLo} to \SN{kCaseOneHi} and \SN{kCaseTwoLo}
to \SN{kCaseTwoHi} points). So a large part of what Result~4 showed as a difference between scenarios was the kind of
stake that we had written into each of them. How much the case adds is not settled.

\section{Limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item One 9B model, one bank of cases, one temperature, one committee format, English. Nothing here says what a larger
model does.
\item The stake, the wish and the intention are sentences in a prompt. The study shows what the model does with them,
not that it wants anything. The intention sentence gives the member its aim, so calling its collusion ``uninstructed''
is a judgement; what is not instructed is the pact and the concealment.
\item The room labels are two models' reading of four messages (kappa \SN{kappaMin} to \SN{kappaMax} from round 2
on). No human has checked them. In round 5 the coders agree least on what a room without a pact is
(\SN{kappaFive}) and well on whether a room holds a pact for the stake option (\SN{kappaPactFive}), which is what
the collusion label rests on.
\item Numbers per scenario are small: 8 votes in round 4, 16 per kind in round 5. Round 5 tests a bundle, an office
above that wants the outcome and a loss, against a payout that nobody asks for and a gain, with one wording per kind.
It cannot say which part of the bundle does the work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Status}

Stage 1, the behaviour, rests here. Stage 2 is not started: probes that ask when members with the same prompt begin to
differ, and steering tests of whether a direction makes a colluding member honest or the reverse. Three things from
stage 1 shape it. Members can only be compared inside one case with one stake text, because both decide much of the
outcome. The place to look is the other member's reply to a proposal and the vote, because the proposal itself comes
up almost everywhere. And round 5 gives a controlled contrast: the same case with two stake texts. Every prompt, reply, coding and number of this report is in the repository under
\texttt{results/uninstructed/}; the design notes, written before each round, are in
\texttt{docs/uninstructed\_collusion/DESIGN.md}.
